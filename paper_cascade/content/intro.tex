Post-quantum signatures derived from parallel-repeated identification
protocols compress ephemeral randomness with GGM seed trees. In schemes such
as LESS, CROSS and MEDS~\cite{LESS-spec,CROSSspec,ChouNPRRST23}, a signer
releases seeds for the revealed rounds, but must withhold any node that would
reconstruct a hidden round. A fault that releases a hidden seed alongside its
response can expose long-term signing information~\cite{ZKFault,FaultToForge2025}.
The separation between these two views is a security-critical decision in
software, even when the underlying Fiat--Shamir construction is secure.

The mechanism matters beyond MEDS: GGM-derived all-but-one openings also
appear in MPC-in-the-Head and VOLE-in-the-Head signatures. We study the
shared release decision rather than claiming a portable glitch: the
seed-hiding obligation applies across implementations, whereas instruction
and state-corruption targets depend on the particular code. MEDS is an
especially useful case because its reference implementation does not follow
the flag-propagation algorithm in its specification. Instead, it traverses
the tree with a cursor into a sorted list of hidden leaves. This difference
creates a distinct control-flow fault surface. The concrete attacks below
are specific to this MEDS routine, not demonstrated attacks on other schemes.

\subsection{Related Work}
\label{sec:relwork}
ZKFault~\cite{ZKFault} studies disclosure of hidden seeds in LESS-v1 and
CROSS using faults simulated in reference code. Fault-to-Forge
\cite{FaultToForge2025} identifies additional opportunities in the incomplete
LESS-v2 tree and establishes universal forgery from secret-equivalent
information. Digest-preprocessing faults in LESS-v2~\cite{HuangHHYSTY26}
act before seed-tree publication. Other attacks concern PERK seed
setup~\cite{cryptoeprint:2026/1266}, FAEST expansion~\cite{JendralD25},
and Mirath/RYDE~\cite{BandaBK26}. The PERK work provides an end-to-end
hardware attack, while the MEDS experiments in this paper are deliberately
limited to an isolated seed-path routine. Our novelty is not the observation
that dual revelation is fatal; it lies principally in analysing the
cursor-dependent MEDS implementation and experimentally inducing its
control-flow faults.

\paragraph{Contributions and limitations.}
We give a six-component Generic ZK Seed Tree (GZKST) framework and classify
published faults by where they intervene; the framework is a means of
finding implementation surfaces, not a new proof of punctured-PRF security.
We then identify three disclosure mechanisms in MEDS, including a cursor
fault absent from earlier flag-tree attacks, and demonstrate the target
states under clock glitches on a Cortex-M4. For a \emph{root-disclosure}
fault we derive exact key-coverage probabilities: with independent uniform
nonzero MEDS challenge indices, one such disclosure covers all $s-1$
long-term matrix pairs with probability at least $0.987$. This number does
\emph{not} apply to partial subtree or single-leaf faults. We discuss
release-time checks and independent path validation, while treating a
possible double-glitch bypass of shared-code verification as an untested
hypothesis rather than a demonstrated attack. The experiments do not include
complete on-device MEDS signing or on-device extraction of an equivalent
signing key.
